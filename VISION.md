# Bite Book — Vision

*A living document — refined as the product and our understanding of it evolve. See `ROADMAP.md` for what's actually being built toward this; see `FEATURES.md` for what exists today.*

## In one sentence

Bite Book is your family's living memory of food — not a place to log what you ate, but a place that makes you *want* to remember it.

## Who this is for

Not the general public, not yet a mass-market product. Bite Book is being built for one real family and the close circle around them — the people someone would actually want to remember meals *with*: a spouse, kids, parents, close friends, colleagues they break bread with. It's designed to feel like something that belongs to that circle, not a service they've signed up for.

## The problem

Photos pile up in a camera roll with no story attached. A meal that mattered — the one Mom made for a birthday, the restaurant that became "our place," the dish a friend still talks about — gets the same treatment as every other photo: buried by chronology, never resurfaced, eventually forgotten. Review apps and recipe apps solve a different problem (where should I eat, how do I cook this); nothing is built specifically to answer "what did we eat, who was there, and why did it matter" — and to hand that back later, when it means something.

## What Bite Book actually is

Not a food log. Not a recipe box. Not a restaurant review site. It's a **memory system with food as the anchor** — the same way a photo triggers a memory, a logged meal should trigger the people, the place, the occasion, the feeling. The product hierarchy that follows from this:

**Capture → Remember → Discover → Reflect** — not **Log → Manage → Rank → Analyze**. The latter is enterprise software. The former is what makes someone open the app again on a random Saturday because they want to, not because they're maintaining a database.

## Product principles

- **AI does the typing, never the deciding.** Every AI-assisted feature (Smart Entry, Ask Your Journal, Smart Search, Clean Up Places) proposes; a person always confirms before anything is saved or changed. Speed of capture should never cost accuracy or trust.
- **Private by default, shared on purpose.** Nothing is visible to anyone but its owner unless they explicitly share it — enforced at the database layer, not just in app code that could have a bug.
- **Low friction in, high warmth out.** Capturing a memory should take seconds (a photo, a sentence, a location tap); what comes back — a story, a photo, a moment resurfaced a year later — should feel worth that little bit of effort.
- **Never lose an accurate meal to duplication or database housekeeping.** The app quietly keeps its own data clean (deduplicating places, catching duplicate entries across family members) without turning that into the user's chore.
- **A feature earns its place by being used, not by being clever.** Manual ranking of 40 meals is clever; nobody does it. Auto-generated "Hall of Fame" is nearly the same information, and people will actually see it.

## What "winning" looks like

Not entry count. Not feature count. The real test: does someone open Bite Book on an ordinary day because they want to check what they ate here last time, revisit a memory from a year ago, or show a friend the story behind a meal — not because they're behind on logging. If the app is only ever opened to *add* something, it has failed at the thing it's actually for.

## Where this is headed (directionally, not a commitment)

A shared, growing archive that gets more valuable the longer a family uses it — not because of any one feature, but because of the accumulated memory itself. AI's job over time is to make that archive easier to *revisit* (surfacing the right memory at the right moment) at least as much as it makes it easier to *create*. The moat isn't the AI or the tech — it's the family's own accumulated food history, which nothing else has.
